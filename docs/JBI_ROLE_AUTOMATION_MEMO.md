# JBI Electrical — solar sales, and what a process can absorb

**For the General Manager, the Managing Director and the board. Not for circulation.**
Prepared 16 September 2026 · Sources: the SPEC solar quote-automation brief (16 Sept), the Nail the
Whale commission design (12 Sept), the Power Meter detail (3 Sept), and Project King of the Mountain
(13–14 Sept). Every figure below is traced to one of those or marked as not known.

## 1. What has been built into SPEC

The Sales Supervisor now exists as a **general role**, not a solar one. Same measures in every
business; the business fills in what it sells — *Solar* at JBI, *New Homes* or *Service Contracts*
somewhere else. A template per industry would have drifted apart inside six months and nobody would
have been able to say which was the real one.

Its measures, drawn from your own documents rather than invented:

| Pillar | Measure | Target | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earnings | Solar quote turnaround within target | 1 day | SPEC KPI, quote-automation brief §1 |
| Earnings | Quote-to-win conversion at target | business to set | Growth template |
| Earnings | Solar revenue at or above target | business to set | Nail the Whale |
| People | Every completed solar job asked for a review | 2 per week | King of the Mountain, Lever 1 |
| People | Team trained and confident to quote without help | — | — |
| Compliance | Every enquiry logged in the client system **and** the job system | 100% | Brief §2, "nothing falls through" |
| Compliance | Quotes issued only from current pricing | — | Brief §6 |
| Safety | The organisational standard | — | — |

Alongside it, SPEC now assesses **every role in the business, measure by measure**, and sorts each
one into: a process can do this · do the legwork for them · needs somebody to look · keep with a
person. It is at `/org/automation` and only the MD, the CEO and the board can open it.

## 2. The principle this is built on, because it is the part worth defending

> **A standard the business already agreed to, which a person cannot reach, is the signal to build a
> process.**

That is the opposite of the usual reason for automating something, and the difference is not
cosmetic. Solar quote turnaround was four days against a one-day standard. The team said one day was
not achievable by a person. **They were right, and being right is the point** — the number came
first, the gap was found by measurement, and the process was built to close it.

The corollary is on the page in as many words, because it matters more to the 40 people who are not
being automated: **a role a process cannot absorb is confirmed as a real job.** Most of JBI is in
that column, and it says so.

## 3. The finding that needs a decision, and it is not the one you would expect

**The Nail the Whale BD scheme is written for a team of three. It is now a team of two.**

The scheme pays each of the three 2% of monthly revenue above the target — team cost 6% of the
excess. With the solar sales manager not backfilled, that is a live commercial question nobody has
answered:

| Monthly revenue | Excess over $900k | Team cost at 6% (three people) | At 4% (two people) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $950k | $50k | $3,000 | $2,000 | $1,000 |
| $1.0M | $100k | $6,000 | $4,000 | $2,000 |
| $1.1M | $200k | $12,000 | $8,000 | $4,000 |
| $1.25M | $350k | $21,000 | $14,000 | $7,000 |
| $1.5M | $600k | $36,000 | $24,000 | $12,000 |

At a $1.1M month that is **$4,000 a month — around $48,000 a year** — and it will resolve itself by
accident if nobody decides. Three ways to go, and they are genuinely different:

1. **Let it fall to 4%.** JBI keeps the difference. Simplest, and the two remaining people may
   reasonably feel the target got harder while their share did not move.
2. **Redistribute to 3% each.** Team cost stays 6%, the two who remain are paid more for carrying
   more, and the poster's arithmetic still works.
3. **Keep 2% each and add the automation as the "third member".** Rhetorically neat, financially
   the same as option 1.

My recommendation is **option 2**, and it is not the cheapest one. The scheme's stated golden rule
is *"JBI wins in every scenario"* — that holds at 3% each, since the cost is still only 6% of money
the business would not otherwise have had. And the thing you are asking two people to do is absorb a
third person's territory while a new process beds in. Paying them option 1 rates to do that is how
you lose one of them, and replacing Anthony costs more than $48,000.

**Whichever you choose, choose it before the next poster goes on the wall.**

## 4. The KPI that will start lying to you

Quote turnaround is about to go from four days to roughly five minutes. The SPEC standard is one
day.

A five-minute process scored against a one-day standard reads **100% green, every month, forever**.
It stops being a measure and becomes decoration — and it is on the scorecard of a role that now
mostly exists to book site visits and close.

Once the automation is live, that KPI needs to be re-cut to measure what a person now actually
controls. My suggestion, in order of usefulness:

1. **Site visits booked within 48 hours of the quote going out.** This is the real bottleneck once
   quoting is instant, and it is entirely human.
2. **Conversion from indicative quote to won job.** The quote arrives in five minutes regardless;
   whether it turns into work is the sales job.
3. **Enquiries with no usable address still unresolved after 48 hours.** The brief's own edge case,
   and the one place a lead silently dies.

Leaving the one-day KPI in place would be the first time SPEC measured something it had already
solved, and every person on that scorecard would notice.

## 5. Other work the same process can absorb, in order of effort

Each of these already has its numbers in Simpro or HubSpot, which is the only thing that makes them
genuinely automatable rather than merely tedious.

| Work | Who does it now | What it becomes | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The review request on every completed job** | 20 a week, by hand, across 4 supervisors and 3 managers | Rides the same job-completion trigger as the morning call list. One build, both outputs | High — the trigger already exists in the brief |
| **Client base coverage** (every client ever invoiced ÷ 1,000) | Assembled by hand for the Power Meter | A query. It is a count over Simpro history | High |
| **Power Meter compilation** (interactions, leads, $ value of leads) | Pulled together for Tuesday's SOG | Computed and waiting before the meeting starts | High — the inputs are already in the two systems |
| **The SOG meeting pack** | Built each Tuesday | Assembled from the above, so the meeting starts on the discussion | Medium — depends on the three above landing first |
| **Google ranking position per town × term** | "Still to close" in the KotM plan — nobody owns it | A weekly automated check, six towns × two terms | Medium — needs a ranking source chosen |

The last one is worth a sentence on its own. King of the Mountain commits **$2,500 a month** to
Google Ads with the outcome "cost per lead and jobs won, reported monthly" — and the weekly ranking
report is listed as *still to close*, with nobody's name on it. That is $30,000 a year of spend with
the measurement of it unassigned, which is the exact failure pattern the case-study email describes
about the previous $3,500 of photography. **Automating the ranking check is cheap; leaving it
unowned is not.**

## 6. What this saves — and what I will not claim

**What I can stand behind:**

- **The BD scheme rebalance: $12,000–$144,000 a year** depending on which option and what revenue
  does. Derived entirely from your own poster's arithmetic. Not a saving so much as a decision about
  where money already committed should go.
- **The solar sales manager package, not backfilled.** You know that figure and I do not. It is the
  largest single number on this page and it belongs in your version of it, not mine.

**What I will not put a number on, and why:**

The review requests, the Power Meter compilation, the coverage calculation and the SOG pack are all
real work being done by real people right now — but **nobody has told me how long any of it takes.**

SPEC is built to refuse here, and it will keep refusing. The savings figure is the one you would
repeat to a board, and one invented hour in that total makes every other number in the system worth
nothing the day somebody checks it. The screen says *"nobody has said how long they take — until
somebody does, SPEC will not put a figure on it."*

**So the ask is small and specific.** At the next SOG, four questions:

1. Anthony — how long does compiling the Power Meter take you each week?
2. Whoever builds the SOG pack — how long?
3. The supervisors — how long does chasing the week's review requests take?
4. Justin — what was the solar sales manager package?

Type those into `/org/automation` and the page does the rest, with a figure you can hand to a board
and defend line by line.

## 7. Before any of this goes near a person

Three things, in order.

**Nobody in a role has been told anything, and nothing here is a decision.** Every verdict on that
page is a proposal with a reason attached, waiting for you. The page says so at the top.

**The page does not leave this room.** It is gated to the MD, the CEO and the board — not managers,
not for their own teams, and no administrator can grant it. Read one desk down it is a page about
whether somebody still has a job, while it is still a proposal. Software cannot stop a screenshot;
the warning is on the page so nobody takes one without having read why not.

**The brief's Section 7 is still open.** The automation cannot be built until JBI supplies the
basic/premium component lists, sizing rules, pricing, rebate treatment, service area, and — the one
most likely to be forgotten — **who owns the morning call list.** That last one is a person's job,
permanently, and it needs a name against it before the first automated quote goes out. An automated
quote nobody follows up is worse than a slow quote somebody does.
